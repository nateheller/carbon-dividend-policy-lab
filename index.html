One global idea, one Alberta pilot

The big idea

The International Sibling Society (ISS) is exploring ways to accelerate progress toward the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals through its 2030 SDG Accelerator. One proposal involves charging for carbon pollution and returning the money to people through a carbon dividend. Higher fuel prices could encourage people to use less fuel, while the dividend could help households cover the added costs.

This lab uses a hypothetical Alberta simulation to explore how a carbon dividend could affect household costs and emissions.

Why Alberta?

Alberta makes the idea manageable to explore. Published household counts and sizes by income group and historical Canadian carbon-charge rates give us a starting point. This Alberta pilot illustrates how the policy could work. It cannot validate ISS’s global projections.

Follow the fuel and the money

  1. 1

    People buy fuel

    Burning gasoline to drive or natural gas to heat a home releases carbon dioxide, a gas that warms the planet.

  2. 2

    A charge raises the price

    The more fuel a household buys, the more it pays in extra charges. Some households may use less fuel.

  3. 3

    Money goes back to homes

    You choose who gets a payment and how much of the money collected goes back to households.

What you can explore. Compare direct charges on gasoline and natural gas, payments to five household income groups, and what could happen to emissions if people use less fuel through 2030. The model uses invented fuel-use profiles and leaves out industry, other fuels, costs passed through goods and services, and the money saved from buying less fuel.

Why the results change

Both policies use the same fuel charge and fuel-use assumption.

Dividend distribution alone does not change emissions in this model. The two policies can return different amounts to the same household, while both assume it buys the same amount of fuel.

Inputs, formulas and limitations

Published inputs. Statistics Canada’s 2023 Survey of Household Spending supplies counts and mean household sizes. Published federal rates (2024–25) and combustion factors are used for gasoline and natural gas. The historical consumer fuel charge was set to zero effective April 2025. All policy settings in this lab are hypothetical.

Assumed inputs. The lab uses five invented annual fuel profiles, holds household counts fixed, and passes the whole fuel charge to buyers. It excludes GST, indirect price effects, other fuels, industry and the value of fuel savings.

Calculation. The assumed 2030 fuel reduction equals response ÷ 100 × price ÷ 100. It phases in by one quarter per year from 2027 to 2030. Each fuel’s remaining volume is multiplied by its published charge rate × price ÷ 80. The total charge across all households × the share returned gives the payout pool. Each plan distributes that same pool using its own eligibility and weights. Direct CO₂ is remaining fuel volume × published combustion factors, summed across households.
